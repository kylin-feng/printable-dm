How to Track Initiative in D&D 5e

Rolling it is easy. Keeping a ten-creature fight moving for six rounds without skipping anyone is the actual skill. Here is the full method, from the roll to the habits that keep combat fast.

The initiative roll, start to finish

When combat begins, everyone rolls initiative: d20 + Dexterity modifier, including the DM for every monster group. Important: monsters of the same kind act together — you roll once for all six goblins and they share a turn. That single rule is the biggest initiative speed-up in the book and most tables ignore it.

Write every result down before anyone acts. If a creature has a modifier to initiative (Alert feat, magic items, the Improved Initiative feature), it applies to the roll — not to a flat 10 in place of rolling unless the ability explicitly says so.

Ties: the Dexterity rule and the house-rule alternative

Officially, ties go to whoever has the higher Dexterity modifier; if those are also tied, roll off or let the players win ties. In practice many DMs run “players before monsters” on ties — it feels generous, costs nothing, and players never notice it is a house rule.

The habits that actually make initiative fast

1. Announce on-deck, every turn

“Aria, you are up — Thorin, you are on deck.” The on-deck call is the single highest-value sentence in combat DMing: it halves the “wait, my turn?” delay and lets the next player plan while the current one acts.

2. Group by roster position, not by creature type

If the wolves act between two players, they are physically between those players on your sheet. Do not sort monsters into a clump just because they rolled together — the order on paper should match the order out loud.

3. Tick the round box every wrap

Rounds are how you measure spell durations and the 1-minute/10-round effects. Tick a box every time the order returns to the top; durations become trivial subtraction.

4. Resolve held and ready actions immediately, not later

A readied action happens the moment its trigger fires — interrupt the current turn, resolve it, continue. If you park it to “deal with it after,” you will forget, and the player will stop ever readying actions.

Common initiative mistakes

  • Rolling per monster. Roll per monster type; the pack of eight wolves gets one score.
  • Skipping delayed/readied participants. Mark them in the notes column the moment the player declares it — see our tracker's held-action markers.
  • Not writing down lair actions. Lair actions act at initiative count 20 (losing ties). Write “Lair: 20” in the roster like a combatant.
  • Erasing HP as numbers. Tally damage instead; arithmetic is where skipped turns are born.

Surprise, and why it is not a round

Surprise is a condition on the surprised creatures' first turn, not a bonus round. Compare stealth rolls against passive Perception; surprised creatures cannot move or act on their first turn and cannot take reactions until that turn ends. Then initiative proceeds normally — the surprised wizard still acts on their count, they just lose the first lap.

The 60-second recap

Initiative is bookkeeping, but bookkeeping is pacing. A fight where everyone knows whose turn it is feels twice as fast as the same fight with three skipped rogues. Roll grouped, write everything, call on-deck, tick rounds — the rest is monsters making interesting choices.
