How to Run a One-Shot

One session, complete story, no campaign commitment — the complete guide to structuring, pacing and landing a single-night adventure.

One-shots are the hardest format in DMing: you get one evening to introduce characters, build tension, and land an ending — with no next session to fix what went wrong. Here's the structure that consistently works.

Start with pregenerated characters

Character creation eats 90 minutes of your only session. Bring 5–6 pregen characters with one hook each baked in — “you owe the innkeeper money,” “you've hunted this cult before.” Players pick in thirty seconds, and every hook is a lever you can pull later. Let players reskin names and pronouns; ownership matters, stats don't.

The three-act one-shot shape

Act 1 (45 min): a strong opening scene. Start in motion — the tavern is already on fire, the auction is already going wrong. Introduce the problem, the stakes, and one memorable NPC in a single scene, then let the players commit.

Act 2 (90 min): investigation plus one fight. Three clues pointing at the truth (three, because players will miss one and misread another), one combat that teaches the villains' trick, and a discovery that recontextualizes the opening — the kind stranger from scene one was behind it all along.

Act 3 (45 min): the climax and a real ending. The showdown, the choice that matters, and — this is what separates one-shots from campaign filler — an epilogue for each character. Two sentences each: what the rogue did with the reward, where the paladin rode next. Endings are why people remember one-shots.

Pacing rules that save one-shots

  • Hard scene cuts. When a scene's point is made, cut — no walking out of buildings, no shopping. "You emerge into the noon market" is a legitimate transition.
  • One fight minimum, two maximum. A one-shot with three fights is a dungeon crawl wearing a costume.
  • Failure advances the plot. If the players lose the Act 2 fight, the villains accelerate their plan — the story moves either way. Never end a scene on a dead stop.
  • The clock is real. Put a visible timer in the fiction — dawn, the tide, the eclipse. One-shots with clocks end on time; one-shots without them end at 2 a.m. in the middle of a corridor.

Prep for a one-shot: one hour, not ten

Use the 30-minute prep routine with one-shot adjustments: the scene list is your three acts, the NPC names double as epilogue material, and the battle math covers your two fights. Fill a combat tracker per fight in advance — table setup is where single-session momentum dies. The session notes sheet still applies: even one-shots deserve a recap of which endings you actually delivered, because players will ask to revisit these characters.

The ten-minute session zero still applies

Even for one night: pitch the tone (heist comedy vs folk horror are different evenings), run lines-and-veils in one sentence, and ask each player what their character wants from tonight. The lightweight version of the session zero checklist takes ten minutes and doubles the emotional payoff.

Where one-shots come from

Steal structures, not plots: any heist movie, any locked-room mystery, any western standoff. A one-shot built on "the party must escort X to Y before Z" is prep-free — the story writes itself from what the players do en route. If you're stuck, open with the reward and end with the bill coming due.
