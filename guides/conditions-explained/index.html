Every D&D 5e Condition, Explained

Fifteen conditions, zero page-flipping: what each one does, what ends it, how they stack — and the table habits that make them effortless.

Conditions are the most looked-up rules in 5e — and the cheapest to master, because each one is two sentences: what it does, and what ends it. Here is the whole set in plain language, grouped by how they actually show up at the table.

The perception conditions

Blinded — can't see; automatically fails checks that need sight. Attacks against it have advantage; its own attacks have disadvantage. Ends when the cause is removed (fog clears, blindness spell ends).

Deafened — can't hear; auto-fails checks that need hearing. Rarely decisive, occasionally devastating (that alarm you didn't hear).

The social conditions

Charmed — can't attack the charmer, and the charmer has advantage on social ability checks with it. Note what it doesn't do: it doesn't grant mind control or bypass violence from other party members. Runs on spell durations.

Frightened — disadvantage on ability checks and attack rolls while the source of fear is in sight, and it cannot willingly move closer to that source. The "willingly" matters: pushed by thunderwave? Fine. Walking in on its own turn? Not allowed.

The action-killers

Incapacitated — the root condition: no actions, no reactions. Several heavier conditions include it.

Paralyzed — incapacitated, can't move or speak, auto-fails Strength and Dex saves, and — the big one — any attack from within 5 feet that hits is a critical hit. Paralysis plus a rogue in melee is a war crime; use it on your players sparingly.

Stunned — paralyzed-lite: incapacitated, can't move, auto-fails Str/Dex saves, attacks against it have advantage — but no auto-crits.

Unconscious — the dying state: everything from paralyzed, plus it drops what it holds, is unaware of surroundings, and shouts don't wake it. Melee hits within 5 ft auto-crit. Ends by regaining HP or being shaken awake as an action.

Petrified — turned to stone: incapacitated, resistant to all damage, immune to poison and disease, auto-fails Str/Dex saves. A petrified character can be carried out of a collapsing dungeon by the party — it's a rescue timer, not a death sentence.

The movement-killers

Grappled — speed becomes 0. If the grappler moves, it can drag you at half speed. Escapes when the grappler is incapacitated, the effect ends, or you're teleported away. A grappled creature can still attack, cast, and shove.

Prone — the self-inflicted one: only crawl or spend half your speed to stand. Melee attacks against a prone creature have advantage, ranged have disadvantage, and its own attacks have disadvantage. Dropping prone is free — tactical against archers, terrible against melee.

Restrained — the web/restraint state: speed 0, attacks against it have advantage, its attacks have disadvantage, and it has disadvantage on Dexterity saves.

The oddballs

Invisible — can't be seen without magic; its attacks have advantage, attacks against it have disadvantage. Invisibility does not silence footsteps — hiding is still a Stealth check.

Poisoned — disadvantage on attack rolls and ability checks. Simple, common, and a reminder to check whether that poison deals damage or imposes the condition — they're different effects.

Exhaustion (six levels) — the ladder: 1) disadvantage on ability checks, 2) speed halved, 3) disadvantage on attacks and saves, 4) hit point maximum halved, 5) speed 0, 6) death. A long rest plus food and drink removes one level; greater restoration (5th level or higher) removes all of them. Exhaustion is the DM's best pacing tool: forced marches, starvation, and drowning all speak it.

How conditions stack

Conditions stack freely — a creature can be grappled, poisoned and frightened at once — but advantage and disadvantage do not: multiple sources of either still amount to one, and one of each cancels out. Track the conditions, apply the net effect once, move on.

Running them without page-flipping

Two habits cover ninety percent of condition play. First: when a condition lands on a character, hand the player the rule — our printable condition cards exist for exactly this; slide the card across, they read it, they own it. Second: keep the one-line summary at your screen — all fifteen fit on two inserts of the DM screen cheat sheet, with each condition's end-condition printed under it.

For the fights where three conditions fly at once, the marks row on the combat tracker has checkboxes for concentration, poison and bloodied — tick them as they land, untick as they save.
