The Session Zero Checklist

Everything to cover in the meeting that decides whether your campaign lasts three sessions or thirty — as a working checklist you can run straight down.

Session zero is the meeting before the game: no dice, no combat, just the table agreeing on what campaign they're all signing up for. Skip it and you don't find out until session four that one player expected politics and another expected dungeon crawls. Here is the checklist, in the order that works at a real table.

1. The pitch (five minutes)

One paragraph: genre, tone, and the shape of the campaign. “Noble intrigue in a collapsing empire, mostly roleplay with set-piece fights, you're all court agents” gives everyone a yes/no decision immediately. If a player's face falls, better now than in week three.

2. Safety tools (ten minutes)

Pick your set and say it out loud: lines and veils (hard bans vs fade-to-black topics), the X-card or a simple “pause” hand signal anyone can use, and an open-door policy — leaving a scene is always fine, no questions asked. This isn't bureaucracy; it's the difference between a tense scene and a bad night.

3. Table rules (ten minutes)

  • Rules authority: DM rulings stand mid-session, rules debated after — say it explicitly.
  • Table tech: phones okay for dice apps? Laptops? PvP — in or out? (Most tables: out.)
  • Attendance: missing players' characters become background NPCs; no XP penalty.
  • Scheduling: cadence (weekly/biweekly), day, start time, and who chases stragglers — agree on the chase mechanism now, while everyone likes each other.

4. Character creation (thirty minutes)

Decide as a table: point buy or rolled stats (rolled in front of witnesses), allowed books, and the party composition conversation — not “we need a healer” but “what does this group look like?” Two warlocks and a bard can work; four loner edgelords with no reason to cooperate cannot, and session zero is where you say so. Require every player to answer three questions: why are you here, whom do you already know at this table, and what do you want from the campaign?

5. Expectations and hooks (ten minutes)

Each player states one thing they want to see (a heist, a redemption arc, dragons) and one thing they'd rather avoid. Write these down — they're your prep priorities for the next three months. Then seed the inter-character bonds: rivals, siblings, debtors, war buddies.

6. Logistics (five minutes)

Where do you play, who hosts, snacks rotation, and how the group communicates between sessions (group chat norms: scheduling yes, rules-lawyering no). Decide what happens when someone can't make it — quorum rules, cancellation cutoff time.

What you walk out with

  • A campaign pitch everyone said yes to
  • Safety tools agreed and understood
  • Characters built with bonds between them
  • A schedule with a real cadence
  • Each player's wants-and-avoids list

Write all of it on one sheet and keep it in your campaign binder — along with a session notes template for every session that follows. The session zero sheet is page one of your campaign's memory.

Common session zero mistakes

Treating it as character creation night. Building sheets takes the whole session and nothing else gets covered — pre-build or budget strict time. Skipping safety tools because “we're all friends.” Friends especially deserve the shortcut. No written record. The agreement you don't write down is the agreement that's re-litigated at session six. And skipping session zero entirely for a one-shot — even a one-shot deserves the ten-minute version: pitch, lines and veils, party bonds.
